English Abstract (up to 1,500 characters)

Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems capture records and structured clinical facts. Still, a third operational layer often remains buried in free text: clinical intent, or what is supposed to happen next for the patient. Follow-up instructions such as “repeat the MRI in two weeks if symptoms persist” may therefore never enter the systems used to schedule, track, and audit care.

CareLoop converts such instructions into structured, source-linked, and reviewable actions. We term this capability Clinical Intent Management (CIM): a reusable, auditable layer between heterogeneous clinical text and the workflows that act on it. CareLoop builds on the PI’s prior neuro-symbolic method for extracting and normalizing follow-up actions and timing.

The key remaining uncertainty is whether this approach transfers reliably from a controlled benchmark to heterogeneous real clinical text and can support a practical EHR workflow. The PoC will extend the method to additional intent fields, validate it on real de-identified clinical notes using a locked test and calibrated review, and demonstrate standards-based integration through FHIR/SMART workflows. The outcome will be a validated pre-pilot prototype suitable for design-partner evaluation and preparation of a real-world pilot.

Current EHR systems often leave patient-specific follow-up intent in free text, outside the structured workflows used to schedule, track, and audit care.   1. Research Plan

Problem, Prior Research, and POC Objectives

The problem. Clinicians constantly decide what should happen next (e.g., a repeat test, a scan, a referral), but write these as sentences, so the systems that schedule, order, remind, and audit rarely receive them in a usable form. As a result, follow-up is frequently missed, delayed, or misinterpreted, a recognized patient-safety and cost problem and a contributor to diagnostic error; ambulatory test-result follow-up and referral completion are well-documented weak points [2,5,11].

What CareLoop is and the method it uses. CareLoop is a Clinical Intent Management (CIM) platform: it makes clinical intent, what should happen next for the patient, computable. While clinical intent appears across many use cases [16], this POC starts with a focused but high-value subcase: extracting clinical follow-up instructions. For a follow-up instruction such as "repeat the blood test in two weeks," it produces a structured, source-linked Actionable Clinical Record (ACR) that includes the action, a normalized due date when the text provides one, the source span it came from, and a confidence flag for review. The method behind this was built in our previous research [1]: a hybrid design in which a learned component finds and links the action and its time expression, an explicit symbolic component normalizes the action and converts relative timing ("in two weeks" → a calendar date), and a selective-prediction step routes low-confidence cases to a clinician. CareLoop is an applied AI system: language models fine-tuned on task-specific labeled data perform extraction; an explicit symbolic layer performs normalization; and general-purpose LLMs support data augmentation and annotation pre-labeling rather than the output path.

Existing market barriers. The initial economic buyer is expected to be hospital or HMO quality, safety, and care-coordination units, while operational users are clinicians and staff responsible for completing follow-up. The unresolved upstream problem is converting follow-up intent already present in clinical text into a reliable workflow object without adding clinician data-entry burden. Adoption depends on three factors: reliable extraction from heterogeneous clinical text, integration with existing EHR workflows, and sufficient precision and provenance to support clinical trust. The PoC directly tests these three barriers.

The POC question is whether the fixed hybrid extraction and normalization approach from [1], extended using established supervised methods, can produce source-supported follow-up records from real de-identified clinical notes at a level of precision and review burden suitable for a pre-pilot system, and whether accepted records can be carried without loss of required information into a standards-based clinical workflow.

Innovation and what the POC extends. The proposed innovation is a system that converts free-text clinical intent into a structured, source-linked Actionable Clinical Record, using hybrid AI extraction, explicit normalization of actions and timing, and standard interfaces for integration into clinical workflows. The design is deliberately hybrid rather than purely generative: large language models provide breadth and language coverage, while an explicit symbolic layer ensures the normalized action, computed date, and source span are exact, so every ACR is auditable rather than a fluent guess. The innovation therefore lies not in task tracking itself, but in creating a reusable, auditable Clinical Intent Management layer between heterogeneous clinical text and downstream care workflows. This positions CareLoop within a broader shift from systems that record what happened, to systems that represent what should happen next [16]. On the controlled 2,000-note benchmark in [1], the completed method achieved action–time Pair F1 of 0.997 on seen and 0.986 on unseen action types, outperforming the evaluated LLM baselines (Pair F1 0.51–0.57). These results establish the technical starting point; the present POC tests whether the approach transfers to heterogeneous real clinical text. It extends the same established approach to an enriched ACR comprising target, actor, condition, and dependency fields, to heterogeneous real clinical text, and to calibrated review and standards-based integration.

Advantages over existing approaches. Follow-up today depends on a clinician manually entering a task or order. Workflow and follow-up tracking systems monitor such tasks well, yet they presuppose that a structured task already exists. Rule-based and NER clinical NLP extracts entities and relations, auditable but stops short of an executable action bound to its timing. General-purpose LLM summarization is flexible, yet its output is neither reliably structured nor traceable to the source; on the PI's CIRCA benchmark, strong LLMs given the gold span recovered all required intent fields only 18–35% of the time [23]. Commercial radiology systems such as PowerScribe One do extract follow-up recommendations into structured fields and pass them downstream [21] but operate inside the radiology reporting workflow. CareLoop targets the gap these leave by recovering intent from clinical text already written across specialties and delivering it as a source-linked record that carries action, timing, conditions, and dependencies, with calibrated review and deterministic mapping into standard workflow resources.

Expected impact. CareLoop is intended to make follow-up instructions currently embedded in text available within existing tracking, reminder, and audit workflows. Prior structured follow-up programs have produced substantial improvements in completion of recommended imaging; the FIND program, for example, increased completion from 30.8% to 50.7% [12]. CareLoop addresses the upstream information gap required to supply those workflows directly from free clinical text; downstream clinical and operational effects would be evaluated in a subsequent real-world pilot.

ROI. Economic feasibility will be assessed at the site level using follow-up volume, baseline non-completion, expected improvement, value per completed follow-up, and software plus human-review costs. The primary output will be a break-even threshold, expressed as the number or percentage of additional completed follow-ups required for a site to cover CareLoop operating costs. Task 6 will estimate these inputs using customer discovery and prospective partner data, with published results such as [12] serving only as external benchmarks. Conservative, base, and higher-impact scenarios will be compared together with per-site, per-volume, and licensing revenue models.

Planned final product. By month 24, the POC will deliver a validated pre-pilot CareLoop prototype with validated extraction of the core follow-up record and evaluated extraction of target, actor, condition, and dependency fields, standards-based EHR integration, and a secure hosted review workflow, along with the market, IP, and ROI evidence needed for a clinical pilot and commercialization. This POC establishes the first focused implementation of the broader Clinical Intent Management vision, which can later extend to additional clinical workflows and intent types.

IP and commercialization plan. A freedom-to-operate review covers the POC assets, and where it supports one, a provisional patent is filed via a patent attorney. The closing output is a go-to-market and licensing plan that names the initial segment and route, along with an asset register documenting ownership and licensing status for the ACR schema, annotation guidelines, trained and calibrated models, integration components, and project-specific derived annotation assets.

External evidence of market need. Follow-up reliability is already recognized as an operational quality problem requiring dedicated workflows. The American College of Radiology reports that up to 10% of radiology reports contain follow-up recommendations and that approximately half of the recommended examinations are not completed; it operates a national Recommendations Follow-Up Improvement Collaborative addressing this gap [4]. CMS separately treats Closing the Referral Loop as a high-priority quality measure [20]. Recent clinical informatics literature describes systematic follow-up management as a workflow spanning identification, communication, tracking through to completion, and outcome assessment. It identifies AI as a means to reduce manual effort across these stages [19]. These signals establish an existing institutional need; Task 6 therefore focuses on validating CareLoop-specific hypotheses regarding buyers, workflows, integrations, pricing, and pilot.

Tasks in Detail

Task 1. Gold-standard data. No public corpus contains the full follow-up ACR labels, so the measurement instrument is a human-labeled gold standard built on de-identified CLIP [13], MIMIC-IV-Note [14], and i2b2/n2c2 2012 [15] (reusing the CLIP action taxonomy and i2b2/THYME TIMEX3 guidelines to reduce cost). Annotation reuses the pipeline from the PI's CIRCA benchmark [23], which harmonized five clinical corpora into 10,011 FHIR-aligned intent records via three-model consensus with human adjudication, so the labeling workflow, crosswalks, and cost model are already demonstrated at scale. A pilot prevalence estimate sets the sampling allocation; if eligible instructions prove sparse, development data are enriched from higher-prevalence components and may be augmented with LLM-generated synthetic communications [22], while a separately sampled natural-prevalence test set of real text is retained for unbiased evaluation. Trained clinical-background annotators mark all eligible instructions on sampled source notes (negatives and non-actionable mentions retained, so detection recall and false-positive rate are measurable) and annotate the semantic fields; the guideline is frozen after a pilot. A stratified subset is independently double-annotated and agreement computed before adjudication, with the clinical advisor resolving disagreements. The target corpus comprises approximately 1,500–2,000 notes and at least 1,000 eligible instructions, including negatives. Target agreement span F1 ≥ 0.85, κ/α ≥ 0.75, and ≥ 0.90 on normalized dates; falling short triggers guideline revision and a second pilot.

Task 2. Engineering adaptation and extended-ACR implementation. Adaptation uses supervised transformer-based clinical language models, with large language models used for candidate generation, annotation pre-labeling, and data augmentation. At the same time, the calibrated hybrid model remains the final extractor so outputs stay auditable. The action–time engine is extended to the remaining ACR fields and adapted to real text using established extraction and normalization techniques; the scientific architecture, learning objective, and temporal-normalization method are fixed, and the PoC addresses implementation, adaptation, and validation rather than development of a new basic method:

Each added element uses an established technique: supervised relation extraction for actor and dependency; span classification and typed NER for condition and target; ontology mapping to SNOMED CT and LOINC for action normalization; TIMEX3 normalization for timing; and selective prediction with probability calibration for confidence and review flags.

Task 3. Locked validation. The corpus splits into train/adaptation, validation, and a single-use test; patients/notes are disjoint. The guidelines, unseen-type vocabulary, configuration, baseline prompts/rules, and confidence threshold are frozen before the test, which is never used for tuning. Experiments cover zero-adaptation transfer, then adaptation, then one locked test; unseen types and source-supported fields; ablations; rule- and fixed-version LLM baselines on identical notes; threshold selection and risk coverage; and clinician workflow with FHIR conformance. On the locked patient-/note-disjoint test set, CareLoop must demonstrate both reliable detection and a practical level of human review. Eligible follow-up instructions must be detected with recall ≥0.80. Among detected records, the selective-prediction system must achieve precision ≥0.95 while automatically accepting at least 70% of records without manual review. Temporal normalization must match the gold-standard date, interval, or event-relative representation under the predefined normalization policy for ≥90% of resolvable expressions; absolute date error will additionally be reported for expressions resolving to a specific calendar date. We will report detection precision/recall/F1, selective coverage, calibration, per-field performance, and performance on predeclared unseen action types with 95% confidence intervals. The integration gate requires that mandatory information be preserved in ACR→FHIR→ACR round-trip tests with no critical FHIR conformance failures.

Task 4. Integration. Reviewed ACRs will be mapped deterministically to a minimum FHIR workflow representation consisting of ServiceRequest/Task with Provenance, preserving source linkage and confidence. A SMART on FHIR application will provide the clinician-facing review surface. Conformance and ACR→FHIR→ACR preservation will be tested using standard FHIR tooling. CDS Hooks, CarePlan, CommunicationRequest, and additional workflow resources will be added only where required by the design-partner pilot specification.

Task 5. Hosted prototype deployment. The prototype is deployed as a containerized, access-controlled hosted service supporting note and FHIR ingestion, model inference, clinician review with source-linked accept/edit/reject controls, provenance display, and standards-based FHIR export. Deployment uses reproducible containers and audit logging on de-identified data; production-scale multi-tenancy and enterprise hardening are outside the PoC scope.

Task 6. Market validation. External evidence establishes follow-up reliability as an existing healthcare workflow and quality problem; Task 6 tests the commercial hypotheses specific to CareLoop. Structured customer discovery with health systems, health plans, care-coordination units, and EHR/workflow vendors will identify the economic buyer, operational user, current workflow and alternatives, integration requirements, acceptable review burden, procurement constraints, pricing basis, and pilot success criteria. We will seek to identify a suitable design partner and obtain an LOI, a pilot commitment, or a jointly agreed pilot specification that defines workflow, integration requirements, and success criteria. Clinical experts will participate as paid consultants evaluating de-identified artifacts; no personal research data will be collected.

Opportunity and Scalability

External evidence supports the market need. Follow-up reliability is already an institutional quality priority, evidenced by the ACR Recommendations Follow-Up Improvement Collaborative [4], the CMS closing-the-referral-loop measure [20], and recent work on AI-supported follow-up tracking [19]. The PoC therefore tests the remaining product-specific hypotheses on adoption, integration, pricing, and pilots.

CareLoop is a Clinical Intent Management (CIM) platform that addresses a missing layer in healthcare IT: clinical intent, meaning what is supposed to happen next for the patient. We have characterized this as a third generation of healthcare IT, moving from systems that record what happened to systems that represent what should happen next [16]. Current EHRs store records and structured facts, but follow-up instructions such as “repeat the blood test in two weeks” or “refer to cardiology if symptoms persist” often remain only in free text and therefore cannot be reliably monitored. CareLoop converts these instructions into structured, auditable, source-linked actions.

The initial product focuses on one well-defined, high-value use case: clinical follow-up instructions. It integrates with existing systems through standards such as FHIR, SMART on FHIR, and CDS Hooks, without replacing the EHR or adding new data-entry burden. The same CIM platform can later support referrals, discharge instructions, radiology recommendations, handovers, and patient messages. This creates a clear path from a focused initial product to a broader platform: start with one high-value workflow, then expand within the same customer and through EHR and workflow-vendor distribution.

The PoC is designed to validate the core CIM technology in a defined use case and, if successful, provide the basis for expansion into additional clinical workflows and a broader Clinical Intent Management platform.

Commercialization Pathways. CareLoop will follow a staged commercialization route. During the PoC, the first objective is to identify a health-system or health-plan design partner, validate workflow fit, and agree on the requirements and criteria for a real-world pilot. After a successful PoC, the first pilot can be initiated with that partner. Successful pilot evidence can then support direct deployment and creation of a reference customer. Scaling would then focus on licensing or OEM integration with EHR and clinical-workflow vendors, allowing the same CIM capability to be distributed across multiple healthcare organizations. Depending on pilot traction and partner interest, commercialization may proceed through HIT licensing or formation of a spin-out. The expected business model is recurring revenue based on site, clinical volume, or enterprise licensing.

Intellectual Property. The scientific foundation comes from our previous research: the hybrid extraction method [1] and the CIRCA harmonized intent dataset and FHIR mapper [23], both PI-owned assets the product will build on. The PoC will create product-specific assets including the ACR representation and schema, annotation guidelines and project-specific annotation layers, trained and calibrated models, evaluation infrastructure, workflow architecture, implementation know-how, and integration components.

A freedom-to-operate and IP review will determine which developments may justify patent protection and which are better retained as proprietary know-how. Defensibility is expected to arise from the combination of protectable methods where applicable, project-specific annotation and calibration assets, validated integration architecture, and implementation know-how. Commercial rights in derived assets will remain subject to the licenses and data-use agreements governing the underlying clinical corpora.

Customers and Follow-on Investment. Initial customers are expected to be health-system quality and safety units, health plans/HMOs, care-coordination functions, and outpatient clinics. These organizations already have follow-up reliability and workflow-performance problems. EHR and care-coordination vendors represent a second customer class that can embed the CIM engine into their products.

The PoC is designed to create the evidence required for the next investment decision: validated performance on real de-identified text, clinician-reviewed outputs, a hosted prototype, standards-based integration, customer-discovery evidence, a prospective pilot or Letter of Intent, and an IP/FTO assessment.

Successful completion would move CareLoop from a research-backed concept to a validated pre-pilot health-tech asset suitable for preparing and initiating a real-world clinical pilot, supporting follow-on funding from digital-health investors, strategic healthcare partners, or EHR and healthcare-software vendors.

Expected Timetable. During PoC, CareLoop validates technical performance, clinician-facing outputs, interoperability, hosted deployment, site-level economics, and IP potential. At the same time, customer discovery identifies design partners and seeks an LOI or agreed pilot specification. In the first 12 months after the PoC, the first real-world pilot is prepared and initiated with that partner; in months 12–24 it is completed, demonstrating operational value and willingness to pay and converting validation into a reference customer. Thereafter, scaling can proceed through enterprise sales, strategic partnerships, and OEM licensing, with expansion into additional clinical-intent workflows.

Feasibility and Risk Reduction. Feasibility is supported by the results of our previous research [1], which demonstrated strong performance relative to conventional LLM baselines. The PoC therefore focuses on transfer, generalization, and productization rather than proving a new scientific principle. CareLoop’s modular architecture strengthens technical feasibility by integrating with existing clinical systems rather than replacing them.

Key risks and mitigation. The principal technical risk is lower accuracy or excessive review burden on heterogeneous real text; if triggered, the initial product will restrict supported instruction classes and route a larger fraction of uncertain records to review. Sparse condition or dependency examples will be treated as enrichment fields rather than core release gates. Integration risk is limited by the minimum FHIR workflow profile, with additional resources added only when required by a pilot. Commercial risk is addressed through customer discovery from project start, explicit testing of buyer and pricing hypotheses, and a target pilot commitment, LOI, or agreed pilot specification before project completion.
